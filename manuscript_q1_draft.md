## Abstract

**Purpose:** To compare ocular surface parameters and spontaneous blinking among patients with thyroid orbitopathy (TO), patients with autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) without orbitopathy and healthy controls, and to examine whether incomplete blinking is associated with meibomian gland loss.

**Methods:** This prospective cross-sectional study included 118 participants (236 eyes): 40 with TO, 42 with AITD without orbitopathy and 36 controls. Assessments included the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI), Schirmer I test, non-invasive tear break-up time (NIBUT), infrared meibography and 240-frames-per-second blink video analysis. Eye-level outcomes were analysed with mixed models clustered by participant and adjusted for age and sex.

**Results:** Adjusted for age and sex, OSDI exceeded controls in AITD without orbitopathy (by 12.2; 95% CI 6.6-17.7) and in TO (26.1; 19.6-32.5). The incomplete blink ratio (IBR) was higher in TO than in controls (19.8 points; 9.0-30.6) and than in AITD (11.9; 1.7-22.0), whereas AITD and controls did not differ. Meibomian gland loss was greater in TO than in controls (6.1 points; 2.3-9.9) and in AITD (4.4; 0.7-8.1). NIBUT and Schirmer did not differ. Each 10-point rise in IBR was associated with 0.80 points more gland loss (0.24-1.35; *P* = 0.005), independent of group, age and sex, and similarly in all groups. Within TO, eyes with eyelid retraction had greater gland loss (5.3 points; 1.4-9.2) but a similar IBR.

**Conclusions:** Ocular surface symptoms were increased in AITD even without orbitopathy, whereas incomplete blinking and meibomian gland loss were confined to TO. Incomplete blinking was associated with gland loss across the whole spectrum, independently of eyelid retraction.

**Keywords:** thyroid eye disease; Graves' orbitopathy; Hashimoto's thyroiditis; incomplete blinking; meibomian gland dysfunction; meibography

## 1. Introduction

Thyroid orbitopathy (TO) is the most frequent extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves' disease and can also occur in chronic autoimmune thyroiditis [1,2]. Autoimmunity against the thyrotropin receptor and the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor activates orbital fibroblasts, which produce glycosaminoglycans and undergo adipogenesis [3,4]. Upper eyelid retraction is the most common clinical sign and is present in more than 90% of patients at some point, while exophthalmos occurs in approximately 60% [5].

Dry eye symptoms are reported by an estimated 65–85% of patients with TO [6,7]. Wider palpebral fissures increase tear evaporation and osmolarity [8], and lacrimal expression of the thyrotropin receptor and inflammatory tear cytokines suggest additional immune-mediated mechanisms [9,10]. Tear film instability, ocular surface staining and higher symptom scores have also been described in patients with Graves' disease or Hashimoto's thyroiditis who have no clinical orbitopathy [11–13], indicating that the ocular surface may be affected before orbital signs appear.

Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is common in TO, and meibomian gland loss increases with disease severity [14–16]. Incomplete blinking is a plausible link between eyelid changes and MGD. About half of all blinks are incomplete in thyroid eye disease [17,18], more than in non-thyroid dry eye [17]. In Graves' upper eyelid retraction most spontaneous blinks are hypometric [19], and blink effectiveness is reduced [20]. In healthy individuals, incomplete blinking reduces tear film stability [21], and in general and clinic populations it is associated with greater meibomian gland dropout [22,23]. It remains unclear at what point along the spectrum of autoimmune thyroid disease incomplete blinking emerges, whether it is explained by eyelid retraction, and whether its relationship with meibomian gland loss is specific to orbitopathy.

We therefore compared symptoms, tear film, meibomian gland loss and blinking dynamics among patients with TO, patients with autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) without orbitopathy and healthy controls. We also examined the association between the incomplete blink ratio and meibomian gland loss, and, within the TO group, the association of eyelid retraction with both.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Study design and participants

This prospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the Oculoplastic Surgery Unit of the Department of Ophthalmology, Prof. Dr. Cemil Taşcıoğlu City Hospital, Istanbul, between September 15, 2024 and March 15, 2025. It adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval date September 9, 2024; decision no. 178). All participants gave written informed consent, including consent for publication of clinical photographs. The study was not prospectively registered.

Three groups were enrolled. The TO group comprised patients with Graves' disease or Hashimoto's thyroiditis and clinically evident orbitopathy, graded according to the European Group on Graves' Orbitopathy (EUGOGO) criteria [24]. The AITD group comprised patients with Graves' disease or Hashimoto's thyroiditis, followed in the Endocrinology Department, without eyelid retraction, soft-tissue involvement or exophthalmos. The control group comprised volunteers with normal thyroid function, no history of thyroid disease and no ocular disease other than refractive error. Eligible participants were aged 18–65 years. Exclusion criteria were use of topical or systemic medication known to affect the tear film, concomitant rheumatological disease, intraocular or adnexal surgery within the previous 6 months, and inability to complete the examination.

Some patients in the AITD group also contributed to a previous retrospective study from our department on systemic composite inflammatory indices in Hashimoto's thyroiditis [43]. That study addressed a different question and shares no outcome variable with the present analysis: it did not include blink video analysis, meibography or EUGOGO grading.

### 2.2. Clinical examination

Age, sex, smoking status, selenium supplementation, type of thyroid disease, thyroid medication, thyroidectomy and radioactive iodine treatment were recorded. Disease activity in the TO group was graded with the Clinical Activity Score (CAS) [25]. A single ophthalmologist performed all examinations under standardised conditions, including best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, non-contact tonometry (TONOPACHY NT-530P; Nidek, Gamagori, Japan), ocular motility and fundus examination. Exophthalmos was measured with a Hertel exophthalmometer, and palpebral fissure width (PFW), margin reflex distances 1 and 2 were measured with a millimetre ruler in primary gaze. Upper eyelid retraction was defined as a margin reflex distance 1 of ≥ 5.0 mm in primary gaze, or visible superior scleral show, or an inter-eye margin reflex distance 1 difference of > 1 mm; the first two criteria allowed bilateral cases to be identified.

Symptoms were assessed with the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) [26]. Tear production was measured with the Schirmer I test without anaesthesia over 5 minutes. Corneal and conjunctival fluorescein staining was graded with the Oxford scheme [27]. Tests were performed from least to most invasive [28].

### 2.3. Non-invasive tear film analysis and meibography

NIBUT and meibography were performed with the Sirius corneal topographer and its Phoenix software (CSO, Florence, Italy) [29,30]. Only acquisitions with a quality index of at least 90% were analysed. The average non-invasive break-up time (NIAvg-BUT) was used throughout. For meibography, the upper eyelid was everted and five infrared images were acquired; the clearest image was analysed. The software calculated the percentage of gland loss within the tarsal area and assigned a grade (0, no loss; 1, <25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 50–75%; 4, >75%) [31]. Meibography meeting the quality criterion was available for 184 of the 236 eyes (78%); these came from 98 of the 118 participants (both eyes in 86, one eye in 12), while no gradable image was obtained in either eye of the remaining 20. The 52 excluded eyes fell below the 90% quality threshold because the participant could not tolerate lid eversion, blinked during acquisition or lost fixation.

### 2.4. Blink analysis

Spontaneous blinking was recorded at 240 frames per second with a smartphone (iPhone 13; Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA) mounted on a tripod 50 cm from the participant [32,33]. Participants sat upright and fixated a distant target in primary gaze. A continuous 5-minute video was recorded; the first and last minutes were discarded to reduce adaptation effects, and the middle 3 minutes were analysed. The same method has been used by our group [34]. Two ophthalmologists masked to group allocation reviewed the recordings in slow motion. A blink was classified as complete when the upper eyelid margin contacted the lower eyelid margin and as incomplete when it did not (Fig. 1). For each participant we recorded the blink rate (blinks/min), and for each eye the number of incomplete blinks per minute. The incomplete blink ratio (IBR) was calculated as incomplete blinks divided by all blinks × 100. Agreement between the two observers was substantial for the binary classification of blinks as complete or incomplete (Cohen's κ = 0.88) and for the continuous measures of blink counts and meibomian gland area loss (intraclass correlation coefficient 0.91; 95% CI 0.86–0.95). Disagreements were resolved by joint review and consensus.

### 2.5. Statistical analysis

Because both eyes of each participant were examined, eye-level data were not treated as independent observations [35,36]. Descriptive statistics and unadjusted group comparisons were performed at participant level, using the mean of both eyes for eye-level variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median [interquartile range]. They were compared with the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Mann–Whitney *U* tests with Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were compared with the χ² test or Fisher's exact test.

Adjusted group differences in eye-level outcomes were estimated with linear mixed-effects models that included group, age and sex as fixed effects and a random intercept for each participant. Participant-level outcomes (OSDI and blink rate) were analysed with linear regression using heteroscedasticity-robust (HC3) standard errors and the same covariates. The association between IBR and meibomian gland loss was modelled in the same mixed-model framework, first unadjusted, then adjusted for age and sex, and then additionally for group. The IBR × group interaction was tested, and models were also fitted within each group. Within the TO group, eyes with and without eyelid retraction were compared with mixed models adjusted for age. Inter-eye agreement was summarised by intraclass correlation coefficients from null mixed models. Analyses were complete-case. A two-sided *P* < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed in Python 3.11 with SciPy 1.17 and statsmodels 0.15.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Participants

The study included 40 patients with TO (80 eyes), 42 with AITD without orbitopathy (84 eyes) and 36 controls (72 eyes) (Table 1). Patients with TO were older than controls (48.6 ± 8.2 vs 40.6 ± 8.6 years; *P* = 0.001), and the proportion of women differed between groups (67.5%, 85.7% and 55.6%; *P* = 0.013). All adjusted analyses therefore included age and sex. Graves' disease accounted for 62.5% of the TO group and 21.4% of the AITD group. Most patients with TO had inactive or mildly active disease (CAS 0–1 in 80.0%), and 24 of 40 (60.0%) had eyelid retraction in at least one eye (30 eyes; bilateral in 6 patients). Hertel values and PFW were higher in TO than in both other groups (both *P* < 0.001).

### 3.2. Ocular surface

OSDI scores increased from controls to AITD without orbitopathy to TO, and all pairwise differences were significant (Table 2). After adjustment for age and sex, OSDI was 12.2 points higher in AITD without orbitopathy and 26.1 points higher in TO than in controls (Table 3). Fluorescein staining (Oxford grade ≥1 in either eye) was present in 30.0% of TO patients, 7.1% of AITD patients and none of the controls (*P* < 0.001).

Schirmer values did not differ between groups. Median NIBUT was lower in both thyroid groups than in controls, but the difference was not significant at participant level (*P* = 0.083) or after adjustment (Table 3). Older age was independently associated with shorter NIBUT (−0.11 s per year; 95% CI −0.19 to −0.03).

Meibomian gland loss was greater in TO (26.7% ± 6.9%) than in AITD without orbitopathy (22.5% ± 7.8%) and controls (20.0% ± 7.3%) (*P* < 0.001; Fig. 2). The adjusted excess was 6.1 percentage points compared with controls and 4.4 compared with AITD. The AITD group did not differ from controls (1.7; 95% CI −2.2 to 5.5). Grade 2 or 3 gland loss was present in 32 of 62 TO eyes (51.6%), 20 of 62 AITD eyes (32.3%) and 14 of 60 control eyes (23.3%).

### 3.3. Blinking

Blink rate did not differ significantly between groups at participant level (*P* = 0.23); after adjustment it was 3.8 blinks/min higher in TO than in controls (95% CI 0.1–7.5; *P* = 0.046), but the overall group effect remained non-significant (*P* = 0.13).

Incomplete blinking differed clearly between groups. The number of incomplete blinks per minute was 5.9 ± 4.7 in TO, 3.7 ± 3.4 in AITD and 2.3 ± 2.5 in controls (*P* < 0.001), and IBR was 41.5% ± 24.8%, 28.3% ± 23.6% and 18.0% ± 16.6%, respectively (*P* < 0.001; Table 2). After adjustment for age and sex, IBR was 19.8 points higher in TO than in controls (95% CI 9.0–30.6; *P* < 0.001) and 11.9 points higher than in AITD (1.7–22.0; *P* = 0.022), whereas AITD did not differ significantly from controls (7.9; −2.5 to 18.4; *P* = 0.14) (Table 3). Inter-eye intraclass correlation was 0.68 for IBR and 0.51 for meibomian gland loss.

### 3.4. Incomplete blinking and meibomian gland loss

In the eyes with meibography, IBR was positively associated with meibomian gland loss (Table 4; Fig. 3A). Each 10-point increase in IBR corresponded to 0.99 percentage points more gland loss (95% CI 0.45–1.53; *P* < 0.001). The estimate was 0.94 (0.39–1.50) after adjustment for age and sex and 0.80 (0.24–1.35; *P* = 0.005) after additional adjustment for group. The association did not differ by group (interaction *P* = 0.70 for TO and 0.82 for AITD), and within-group estimates were similar in direction and size (TO 0.65, AITD 0.91, controls 0.96 per 10 points) although estimated with less precision. The number of incomplete blinks per minute showed a weaker association after adjustment for group (0.33 percentage points per incomplete blink/min; 95% CI −0.02 to 0.68; *P* = 0.065). At participant level, the pooled Spearman correlation was 0.30 (*P* = 0.003; n = 98).

In exploratory analyses, neither blink rate nor IBR was significantly correlated with OSDI, NIBUT, Schirmer or Hertel values in the pooled sample. IBR correlated weakly with PFW (ρ = 0.18; *P* = 0.049), but not within any single group. IBR was inversely correlated with OSDI in the AITD group only (ρ = −0.44; *P* = 0.004).

### 3.5. Eyelid retraction in thyroid orbitopathy

Within the TO group, eyes with eyelid retraction had greater meibomian gland loss than eyes without retraction (29.1% ± 10.0% vs 24.1% ± 6.1%; age-adjusted difference 5.3 percentage points, 95% CI 1.4–9.2; *P* = 0.008). IBR was slightly higher in eyes with retraction, but the difference was not significant (43.7% ± 25.1% vs 40.1% ± 29.3%; adjusted difference 9.6 points, −0.7 to 20.0; *P* = 0.068), nor was the number of incomplete blinks (6.2 ± 4.9 vs 5.7 ± 5.2 per minute; adjusted difference 1.1, −0.4 to 2.7; *P* = 0.15) (Table 4; Fig. 3B and 3C).

## 4. Discussion

In this prospective study, patients with autoimmune thyroid disease reported more ocular surface symptoms than controls even without orbitopathy, but incomplete blinking and meibomian gland loss were increased only once orbitopathy was present. Incomplete blinking was associated with meibomian gland loss across all three groups, independently of age, sex and group, and the strength of that association was similar in each group. Within TO, eyes with eyelid retraction had more gland loss, but their incomplete blink ratio was not significantly higher.

The elevated OSDI score in AITD without orbitopathy agrees with earlier reports of symptoms and tear film changes in Graves' disease without exophthalmos and in Hashimoto's thyroiditis [11–13]. In contrast with those studies, NIBUT and Schirmer values in our AITD group did not differ significantly from controls after adjustment. NIBUT was shorter in older participants, and the thyroid groups were older than the controls, so part of the crude NIBUT difference was attributable to age. Corneal staining was uncommon in AITD (7.1%), and gland loss in AITD did not differ from controls, although the confidence interval does not exclude a difference of up to about 5 percentage points. Altin Ekin et al. reported greater meibomian gland dropout in Hashimoto's thyroiditis without orbitopathy [13]; our AITD group, 79% of whom had Hashimoto's thyroiditis, may have been too small to detect a difference of that size. Taken together, these findings suggest that in AITD without orbitopathy the symptomatic burden precedes measurable structural change at the eyelid margin.

The mean IBR of 41.5% in our TO group is close to the 51–53% reported by Park and colleagues in thyroid eye disease [17,18]. It is also consistent with the observation that most spontaneous blinks are hypometric in Graves' upper eyelid retraction [19] and that blink effectiveness is reduced in Graves' orbitopathy [20]. Blink rate, by contrast, was only modestly higher in TO and the overall group effect was not significant, which is in keeping with the absence of a blink-rate difference reported with long-duration magnetic search coil recording [41] and argues that the quality rather than the frequency of blinking is what changes in TO.

The greater gland loss in TO is consistent with previous meibography studies in Graves' orbitopathy [14–16]. Schirmer values were similar across groups, which fits the predominantly evaporative pattern described in TO [8,37] and the role of the meibomian glands in tear film stability [38,39]. Gland loss increases with age in healthy eyes [31], but the excess in TO persisted after age adjustment.

The association between IBR and gland loss was the most consistent finding of this study. It persisted after adjustment for group, age and sex, it did not differ between groups, and its direction was the same within each group. This agrees with studies in general and dry eye populations in which incomplete blinking was associated with greater meibomian gland dropout, poorer tear film stability and dry eye [22,23]. A plausible mechanism is that an incomplete blink does not fully compress the tarsal plate, so meibum expression is reduced, which may promote ductal stasis and, over time, gland atrophy [39]. The reverse direction is also possible: gland dropout and an unstable tear film may alter blink behaviour. The cross-sectional design cannot separate these explanations, and the effect size was modest, at about 0.8 percentage points of gland loss per 10 points of IBR. That the association was independent of group, and similar in controls and patients, suggests a general mechanism at the eyelid margin rather than a thyroid-specific one.

Within TO, eyes with eyelid retraction had more gland loss, but their IBR was only non-significantly higher, and IBR was not correlated with Hertel values or PFW within the TO group. This argues against the simplest mechanical explanation, in which incomplete blinking in TO would follow directly from the widened fissure and the greater excursion required for closure. Park et al. likewise found no correlation between incomplete blinking and palpebral fissure height or proptosis in thyroid eye disease [18]. Eyelid changes that static measurements of fissure width or globe position do not capture, such as the reduced blink effectiveness described by Guimarães et al. [20], may be more relevant. Because 18 of the 24 patients with retraction were affected unilaterally, the gland-loss comparison is largely within-patient and is therefore less susceptible to confounding by systemic factors.

These results have practical implications. Ocular surface symptoms should be assessed in patients with autoimmune thyroid disease even when there are no orbital signs, although our data suggest that structural meibomian gland change is unlikely at that stage. In established TO, where both incomplete blinking and gland loss are increased, blink completeness is a plausible target: blinking exercises reduce the incomplete blink ratio and improve tear film parameters in dry eye [42], and their effect in TO could be tested prospectively.

This study has several limitations. First, it was cross-sectional, so causal or temporal relationships cannot be established. Second, the groups differed in age and sex. We adjusted for both, but residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, the TO group consisted mainly of patients with mild, largely inactive disease, which limits generalisation to moderate-to-severe or active TO. Selenium supplementation was common in TO and could not be analysed separately. Fourth, meibography was unavailable for 52 of 236 eyes (22%), which may have introduced selection bias. Fifth, smartphone video at 240 frames per second has been shown to be feasible for blink analysis [32] but has not been validated against a reference standard, and blink classification remains observer-dependent. Sixth, we did not measure lipid layer thickness, tear osmolarity, meibum expressibility, thyroid hormone or antibody levels, or disease duration, and the study was not prospectively registered or preceded by a formal sample size calculation. Finally, the exploratory correlations were not corrected for multiple testing, and the inverse correlation between IBR and OSDI in the AITD group should be interpreted with caution.

## 5. Conclusions

Patients with autoimmune thyroid disease reported more ocular surface symptoms than healthy controls even in the absence of orbitopathy, whereas increased incomplete blinking and meibomian gland loss were confined to patients with thyroid orbitopathy. Incomplete blinking was associated with meibomian gland loss across the whole spectrum, independently of group, age and sex, and this association was not explained by eyelid retraction. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether incomplete blinking contributes to gland loss and whether improving blink completeness can slow it.
